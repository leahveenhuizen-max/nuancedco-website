## 1. Overall Course Facilitation Principles

The Unmasked Life is an educational course. The role of the facilitator is to teach, hold space for reflection and provide accurate framing. It is not to provide therapy, diagnose, treat or counsel.

**Core principles:**

- Education first. Every session delivers information before processing.
- Normalise without pathologising. The aim is accuracy, not alarm.
- Hold the educational frame consistently. If the group moves into processing, gently redirect to reflection within scope.
- Do not attempt to resolve distress in a group setting. Acknowledge it, name the support pathway, move on.
- You are not responsible for how participants receive information. You are responsible for how you deliver it.
- Participants do not need to be fixed. They need to be understood.
- Silence is allowed. Not every moment needs filling.
- One facilitation voice. If others are in the room, only the facilitator responds to clinical-sounding questions.

## 2. Educational Scope and Boundaries

**This course is:**
- Research-informed ADHD education for late-diagnosed adults
- Reflective and practical in focus
- Facilitated by a neurodivergent consultant and registered nurse
- Within the scope of education, not therapy

**This course is not:**
- A therapy group or counselling programme
- A diagnostic tool or process
- A substitute for mental health treatment
- Crisis support or mental health first aid
- A space for individual case formulation or clinical assessment

**Scope boundaries in practice:**

| Participant says | Appropriate response |
|---|---|
| "I think I have trauma from this" | "That makes sense. Many people recognise trauma responses after late diagnosis. A trauma-informed therapist can support that work. This course can help you understand the ADHD layer." |
| "Can you tell me if my medication is right?" | "I cannot advise on medication. That is a conversation for your prescribing doctor. What I can help with is understanding how ADHD affects your day-to-day and what supports exist." |
| "I think I might not actually have ADHD" | "Diagnosis doubt after late diagnosis is really common and worth naming. This course does not diagnose or re-diagnose. If you have concerns about your diagnosis, your GP or assessing clinician is the right person." |
| "My GP doesn't believe me" | "That is unfortunately common for late-diagnosed adults. I can point you toward resources on self-advocacy and how to approach that conversation." |

## 3. Managing Group Discussion Safely

- Set group agreements at the start of Week 1 catch-up: confidentiality, speak for yourself, no unsolicited advice, step up and step back.
- Do not encourage participants to share detailed personal history. Prompt reflection, not disclosure.
- If a participant shares something deeply personal, acknowledge briefly, do not explore further in the group, and follow up privately or signpost.
- Redirect group tangents with: "That is a really important thread. Let us hold it and come back to it individually. For now, can I bring us back to..."
- If the group energy drops or becomes heavy, use the check-in prompt: "Where are you sitting right now? One word is fine."

---

## 4. What to Do If Distress Arises

**In the group:**
1. Acknowledge what was shared without expanding it. "Thank you for naming that."
2. Pause the content if needed. "Let us take a moment."
3. Offer the scope reminder gently. "This course is here to support your understanding. If this is bringing up something that needs more than education, that is okay and normal. I will share some support resources at the end of today."
4. Continue the session unless the person is in acute distress.

**If a participant appears to be in acute distress:**
1. Pause the session.
2. Address them directly, calmly and without alarm: "It looks like this has landed somewhere important for you. Are you okay to continue, or do you need to step away for a moment?"
3. Do not attempt to manage the distress therapeutically.
4. Provide crisis resources: Lifeline 13 11 14, Beyond Blue 1300 22 4636.
5. Follow up privately after the session.
6. Document the incident internally.

**Post-group debrief for facilitator:**
After each session, note: anything that was outside scope, any participant who may need follow-up, your own wellbeing check.

## 5. Difference Between Education and Therapy

This distinction matters for your own clarity as facilitator and for participants who ask.

| Education (within scope) | Therapy (not within scope) |
|---|---|
| Teaching ADHD frameworks and concepts | Diagnosing, treating or formulating |
| Normalising and reframing patterns | Processing trauma or grief therapeutically |
| Providing practical tools and strategies | Providing ongoing psychological support |
| Facilitating reflection | Conducting counselling or clinical assessment |
| Signposting to services | Providing crisis intervention |

When a participant crosses the line toward expecting therapy, name it gently: "I want to make sure I am being useful and staying within what I can genuinely offer. What I can give you here is the educational framework and support to understand your brain. For the deeper processing work, I would encourage you to work with a therapist who understands neurodivergence."

## 6. Recommended 60-Minute Weekly Catch-Up Structure

| Time | Section | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 to 0:05 | Welcome and grounding | Brief check-in, one word or one sentence |
| 0:05 to 0:15 | Week recap | Facilitator summarises the core teaching from the week |
| 0:15 to 0:30 | Participant reflection | Open prompt from the workbook or QR card |
| 0:30 to 0:45 | Discussion | 2 to 3 guided questions, facilitator manages direction |
| 0:45 to 0:55 | Application | What is one thing you are taking from this week? |
| 0:55 to 1:00 | Close, preview and scope reminder | Next week preview, support resources reminder |

**Notes:**
- Keep the welcome short. Late ADHD brains often arrive scattered. Give them 5 minutes to land.
- Do not skip the recap. Participants may not have completed the written content before attending.
- Application questions are more useful than insight questions. "What will you do?" beats "How did that make you feel?"
- End on time. Respect for participants' time is also modelling healthy self-structure.

## 7. Week 1 Facilitation Plan: The Late Diagnosis Disruption

### Session Purpose
Help participants begin to understand what late ADHD diagnosis does to the story they have been telling about themselves, and make space for the emotional complexity of that without requiring resolution.

### Learning Outcomes
By the end of this session, participants should be able to:
- Name at least one pattern they are now seeing differently
- Understand that the emotional response to late diagnosis is varied and valid
- Identify one way executive function may have affected their life
- Describe the difference between self-blame and understanding

### Five-Minute Opening
"Welcome to Week 1. I want to start by saying something simple: you do not have to feel a certain way about any of this. Some of you may feel relieved. Some may feel furious. Some may feel nothing yet. All of those are legitimate responses to new information landing in an old life. This week we are going to start with the disruption itself."

Check-in prompt: "In one word, what did late diagnosis bring up for you first?"

### Key Teaching Recap
- Late ADHD diagnosis often triggers a life review: looking back through everything with new information
- Patterns that looked like laziness, inconsistency, sensitivity, messiness or failure may now look different
- This does not mean ADHD explains everything; it means there is better language for patterns that were always there
- Executive function affects: task initiation, working memory, time awareness, emotional regulation, planning and follow-through
- The aim of Week 1 is not to rewrite the past. It is to understand it with more accuracy and less shame.

### Suggested Participant Questions
- "What is one thing from your past that is making more sense now?"
- "What old label are you starting to question?"
- "What did you feel when you first received your diagnosis? Has that changed?"
- "What were you told about yourself that might have been an ADHD pattern being misread?"

### Optional Discussion Prompts
- "What does relief feel like in your body? What does grief feel like?"
- "What would you tell a younger version of yourself if you could?"
- "What is the hardest part of seeing the patterns now?"

### Areas That May Produce Distress
- Grief for education, career, relationships or time
- Anger at systems, family members or clinicians who missed the diagnosis
- Shame about what they were told about themselves
- Overwhelm at the volume of what is being seen differently at once

### Scope Reminders
- This course does not diagnose, re-diagnose or validate a diagnosis.
- If a participant expresses significant distress about missed diagnosis, acknowledge it briefly and direct them to individual support.
- Do not explore traumatic histories in the group.
- If participants ask what their specific patterns "mean" diagnostically, return to education: "What I can offer is the framework. Your clinician is the right person for anything diagnostic."

### What to Validate

Leah should actively validate:
- The experience of the life review: "That is a very common part of receiving a late diagnosis. Looking back is something many people go through."
- Emotional complexity: "Relief and grief and anger can all show up in the same week. That is not contradictory. That is a reasonable response to important information arriving late."
- The weight of old labels: "Being told something about yourself for years does not make it true. It makes it sticky. That is different."
- Difficulty with the course material: "If any of this is landing heavily, that is allowed. You do not have to push through it to stay in the course."
- Absence of strong feelings: "Some people do not feel much yet. That is also fine. Understanding can take time to settle."

### What Not to Attempt in the Group
- Do not facilitate grief processing
- Do not explore childhood trauma
- Do not compare diagnoses between participants
- Do not offer clinical interpretation of participant experiences
- Do not promise that understanding ADHD will resolve all past difficulties

### Optional Non-Verbal Participation

For participants who find it difficult to speak in a group or who need an alternative:
- Offer a chat function or a shared notes document if the session is online
- Allow participants to respond with a number (1 to 5) or a colour rather than words for check-in
- Permit written reflection during discussion time: "If you would rather write than speak right now, that is completely fine. You can share if you want to, later."
- Do not press for verbal responses. Silence is a valid participation mode.
- For TikTok Live format: use the comment section as a non-verbal option. People can comment words or numbers without speaking.

### Closing Reflection
"This week asked you to sit with something that may have been moving for a long time. You do not have to have it sorted by the end of this session or this week. The aim is to start seeing it more clearly. That is enough for now."

### Next-Week Preview
"Next week we go into masking. If you have ever spent significant energy appearing fine, appearing capable, appearing consistent or appearing like someone you were not quite sure you actually were — that is what we are exploring. Week 2 is about the cost of that, and what it means to start putting some of that weight down."

### Three TikTok Live Topics from Week 1

1. **"The life review after late ADHD diagnosis"**
   What happens when you start looking back with new information. Why everything feels like it is reorganising itself. How to do that without falling apart.

2. **"What if laziness was executive dysfunction all along?"**
   The most common misreads of ADHD patterns in adults. What executive function actually affects. Why being told to "just try harder" was always the wrong answer.

3. **"The emotional chaos of being diagnosed late"**
   Relief and grief and anger can all show up in the same week. That is not a problem. That is a reasonable response to finding out something important was missed.

### Five Short-Form Content Prompts from Week 1

1. "Late ADHD diagnosis often brings a life review. Everything you thought you understood about yourself starts asking to be re-examined. That is not a breakdown. That is information arriving late."
2. "You were not lazy. You were dysregulated, undersupported and working twice as hard without understanding why."
3. "Grief after late diagnosis is not the same as self-pity. It is a valid response to information that arrived too late to prevent real losses."
4. "Executive function is not about effort or intelligence. It is about the neurological processes that help you initiate, organise, remember and follow through. Late-diagnosed adults often have years of evidence that these processes work differently."
5. "The aim of understanding your ADHD is not to fix yourself. It is to understand yourself with more accuracy and less shame."

### Five Week 1 Quote-Card Lines

For use across Instagram, TikTok and Facebook as standalone text cards. These should be set in Nuanced Co. brand design.

1. "You were not failing at being a person. You were a brain working hard without the right explanation."
2. "Late diagnosis does not change your past. It changes how clearly you can see it."
3. "Relief and grief after diagnosis are not opposites. They are both a reasonable response to the same thing."
4. "Understanding your ADHD is not an excuse. It is a more accurate explanation than the one you were given."
5. "The life review after late diagnosis is not self-pity. It is what happens when accurate information arrives late."

### Week 1 Common Participant Questions and Suggested Responses

**"Is it normal to feel worse after diagnosis?"**
"Yes. This is a recognised part of the late-diagnosis experience. New information can temporarily increase distress before it reduces it. The life review is real and it can be heavy. It usually settles over time, and having accurate language for what you are experiencing tends to help."

**"Does this mean everything in my past was ADHD?"**
"Not necessarily. ADHD may help explain some of the patterns. Some things will make more sense with this understanding. Others may not be connected. The work of Week 1 is noticing without requiring certainty."

**"I feel guilty that I feel relieved."**
"You do not need to feel guilty about relief. Understanding why things have been hard is not a betrayal of anyone. Relief and grief can both be true at the same time. You do not have to choose."

**"I am angry. Is that okay?"**
"Anger is a completely legitimate response to finding out that something important was missed. It does not need to be softened or resolved quickly. What I would say is: try to point the anger at the right target, which is usually the systems that failed to identify this, not yourself."

**"What if I am not ready to look at all of this yet?"**
"Then you do not have to. This course has no enforcement mechanism. You can work at the pace that feels safe and useful for you. Some people need to sit with the diagnosis for a while before the review stage begins. That is fine."

## 12. Common Participant Questions

**"Does this mean I have always had ADHD?"**
Late diagnosis means the pattern was always there. The recognition is new. You do not need a response to this — you can simply affirm: "The research strongly supports that ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition present from early life. What changes at diagnosis is the understanding, not the condition."

**"Is it normal to feel worse after diagnosis?"**
Yes. This is documented. New information can temporarily increase distress before it reduces it. The life review is real. Grief is real. The aim is not to rush past it but to understand it clearly.

**"What if my diagnosis is wrong?"**
Diagnosis doubt is common, especially for adults who were told for years that they were fine, lazy, dramatic or difficult. This course does not re-diagnose. If this is a strong concern, encourage them to return to their assessing clinician.

**"Can ADHD cause [specific symptom]?"**
Direct factual ADHD questions are within scope to answer from an educational perspective. Anything that begins to sound like individual clinical formulation ("could my ADHD be causing my relationship to collapse") should be redirected to appropriate professionals.

**"Will I always feel this way?"**
You can offer: "Understanding your ADHD does not fix everything immediately, but it does give you better tools for understanding what is happening and what support might actually help. Many late-diagnosed adults describe a significant shift over time."

**"Why wasn't I diagnosed earlier?"**
This is legitimately painful and worth acknowledging. "Diagnostic pathways in Australia and globally have historically missed a significant number of adults, particularly women, people who masked effectively and those without hyperactivity. That failure belongs to the systems, not to you."

## 13. Key Myths to Correct

| Myth | Correction |
|---|---|
| ADHD is just about hyperactivity | ADHD affects executive function, emotional regulation, attention, memory, time and sensory processing across multiple domains |
| Adults grow out of ADHD | Presentation changes with age and coping strategies, but the neurological difference remains |
| If you can focus on things you enjoy, you do not have ADHD | Interest-based nervous systems are a documented feature of ADHD, not evidence against it |
| ADHD is over-diagnosed | The research suggests ADHD is under-identified in adults, women and people who mask well |
| People with ADHD are just disorganised | Executive dysfunction is neurological, not a character trait or a result of poor effort |
| Medication is the only treatment | Medication is one tool. Education, environmental design, support structures and self-understanding are also documented to improve functioning |
| You should be able to manage by now | Without accurate understanding and appropriate support, managing ADHD becomes increasingly costly over time, not easier |

## 16. Closing and Referral Language

**Closing a session:**
"We have covered a lot this week. I want to leave you with this: you do not need to have resolved any of it by Friday. Understanding is allowed to take time. If anything from today brings up something that feels bigger than this course can hold, that is okay and that is important information. I will always point you toward the right support."

**Referring to external support:**
"What you are describing sounds like something that would benefit from one-on-one support. I can point you toward some resources for finding ADHD-affirming practitioners in Australia. This course can run alongside that work, but it is not a substitute for it."

**Referring to crisis support:**
"If things feel urgent or unsafe, please do not wait until next week. Lifeline is available 24 hours on 13 11 14. Beyond Blue is on 1300 22 4636. You can also text 0477 13 11 14 if calling is hard."

**When someone is in the wrong programme:**
"I think what you need right now might be more than this course can provide, and I want to make sure you get the right support. This course is educational and reflective in nature. What you are describing sounds like it needs a qualified clinician. Would you like help identifying where to start with that?"
