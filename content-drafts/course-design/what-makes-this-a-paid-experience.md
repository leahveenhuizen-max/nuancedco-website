# What Makes This a Paid Experience
## The framework Week 1 needs — and what it currently does not have

**Date:** 19 June 2026  
**Purpose:** Define what The Unmasked Life™ Week 1 needs to justify its cost, sustain engagement, and feel categorically different from free content

---

## The core problem stated plainly

Good writing about ADHD is freely available. Accurate, compassionate, research-informed content on late diagnosis, identity disruption, executive function, and the grief of late diagnosis exists on blogs, podcasts, YouTube channels, and Reddit. A learner who has been recently diagnosed has likely already consumed significant amounts of it.

What justifies paying for a course is not the content. It is the experience of the content. And that experience must do something the free version cannot:

- Guide the learner through emotionally significant material at a pace that is appropriate for their state
- Create outputs that represent their specific understanding, not a general understanding
- Produce a genuine shift — in self-knowledge, in vocabulary, in capacity to act — that the learner can point to at the end of the week
- Make the learner feel that this was designed for *them*, not for an imagined generic adult with ADHD

Currently, Week 1 does not do these things. The content is excellent. The experience is a reading experience.

---

## What Week 1 currently does not have

### 1. Designed moments

A designed moment is a point in the page where something happens that the learner did not expect — something that stops the scroll, signals weight, or creates a visceral response before any prose explains why. The current page has no designed moments. The prototype has one approximate designed moment (the recognition card grid). Neither page has a moment where the learner feels the content before they understand it intellectually.

For Week 1, the designed moment that is obviously available but unused: **the reinterpretation of the past**. The core concept — that information arriving late rewrites what you thought you knew about yourself — is intrinsically visual. A label appearing and then being renamed. A memory described and then redescribed. The page could do this. It currently describes it in prose.

### 2. Visual teaching

Visual teaching means using the visual surface of the page to communicate what the words are saying, not just to display the words. The current executive function section says "executive function is your brain's management system: task initiation, working memory, time awareness, emotional regulation..." — and then lists these in prose. The prototype wraps them in flex boxes. Neither page uses the visual surface to *show* what executive function disruption looks like — the experience of starting a task and not being able to, the feeling of time disappearing, the disconnect between knowing what to do and doing it.

Visual teaching does not require complex animation. It requires a design decision that the concept is worth visualising, not just describing.

### 3. Guided progression

The learner should never be uncertain about where they are and what comes next. Currently, a learner on Week 1 can see the section labels (Start here, the accordion headers, card labels) but cannot see the shape of the whole. They cannot tell: how long will this take, what am I doing right now relative to the whole week, have I covered the important things, is there more I must do or are these options.

Guided progression means the learner has a visible path. The prototype's seven-dot lesson map is the right instinct but it is too small and does not update to reflect the learner's actual progress through the page. A sticky stage indicator that genuinely updates — "You are in Understand. Next: Recognise." — would change the learner's orientation completely.

### 4. Personalisation

Neither version personalises anything. Every learner sees the same content in the same order at the same pace. The course is designed for an adult with a recent ADHD late diagnosis, but it treats all such adults identically. In reality: some learners are arriving on the day of their diagnosis, some are six months post-diagnosis and still processing, some are in crisis, some are intellectually engaged and emotionally defended, some are in tears. The page cannot currently distinguish between them or respond to them differently.

Meaningful personalisation for a self-paced course does not require a backend. It requires entry points. A pre-week question ("How are you arriving today? Which of these feels most live right now?") that routes the learner to a starting point appropriate to their state would be significant.

### 5. Learner-generated outputs

The most important missing element. Currently, a learner can finish Week 1 and have nothing to show for it — no written record of their recognition patterns, no saved Life Review Map, no named takeaway, no statement they produced about what changed.

Learner-generated outputs matter for three reasons:
- They prove the course was worth paying for (the learner has something)
- They deepen the learning (writing is not a record of understanding, it creates understanding)
- They are the product of the session, not the reading material

The outputs required for Week 1:
- Recognition patterns selected (which of the 8 cards)
- Life Review Map — at least one completed step (written by the learner)
- A single takeaway statement ("The one thing I am carrying forward from this week")

All three are achievable today. The recognition cards exist in the prototype. The Life Review Map textarea exists in the prototype. The takeaway input exists in the prototype. None of them are in the production page.

### 6. Meaningful interaction

Meaningful interaction is interaction that changes what the learner knows or creates something they didn't have. The current page's interactions (accordion toggle, milestone tick, timer, format switch, display toggle) are all either navigational or decorative. They manage the experience but do not contribute to the learning.

Meaningful interactions for this content:
- Selecting recognition patterns → seeing them named back
- Writing into the Life Review Map → watching the step mark as complete
- Ticking the milestone → triggering a specific, emotionally resonant confirmation (not just a checkmark, but a sentence that acknowledges what the learner just did)
- Choosing an integration option → receiving a brief prompt specific to that choice

### 7. Emotional pacing

The emotional weight of Week 1 varies dramatically. "Executive function is your brain's management system" is educational. "Grief is not the same as self-pity" is personally significant. "What emotions have come up for you since your diagnosis?" is potentially destabilising. The current page treats all of these at identical pace and volume.

Emotional pacing means the design signals when to slow down, when something heavy is arriving, and when a moment of breathing space has been created. Visually this could be as simple as a full-width quiet block before the grief content — white space, a single sentence, no card borders. The design equivalent of a pause.

It also means not asking heavy questions in the same breath as administrative instructions. The current "Recognise" section puts three workbook prompts about grief and self-blame inside a card that opens with "Before moving into the reframe and the exercise, take a moment to notice..." — good instruction, but visually identical to the card that listed course resources above it.

### 8. Continuity across the week

Week 1 is a self-contained island. There is no evidence within the page that the learner's engagement here will be relevant to Week 2. The "Coming in Week 2" teaser describes the next topic but does not connect to what the learner just did. If the learner produced a Life Review Map step or named a recognition pattern, Week 2 should acknowledge this — "You identified time blindness as a pattern last week. This connects directly to what we cover in executive function in Week 3."

Continuity is not technically complex. It is architecturally deliberate. The current architecture treats each week as a separate document. The learner experience treats them as four chapters of the same story.

### 9. Role of video

The video is the most important missing element in both current and prototype pages. Everything else on both pages is text-with-interaction. The video is the instructor's presence. It is the emotional register-setter for the week. It is the moment the learner hears a real voice speaking to them.

Without the video, the course is a premium self-help workbook. With the video, it can become a course. The video does not need to be long (10–15 minutes for Week 1), cinematic, or expensively produced. It needs to: be addressed to this specific learner, acknowledge what they might be feeling arriving at Week 1, introduce the key concepts in a way that shows Leah's expertise and humanity, and give the learner explicit permission to go at their own pace.

The video design decisions to make before production:
- Will it be Leah to camera, or narrated over visuals?
- Will it be chaptered so learners can skip to specific concepts?
- Will the transcript be built into the page from the start?
- Will learners who cannot watch (sensory sensitivity, no audio access) have an equivalent written version — not just a speed-read?

### 10. Role of worksheets

The Companion Guide ("Grieving the Life You Thought You Had") and the standalone Life Review PDF are the right products. The problem is that they are mentioned as downloads, not introduced as essential components of the week's learning.

A worksheet is not a bonus. For a learner doing emotionally significant work on their own, the worksheet is where the learning actually happens. It needs to be introduced as: "This is what you do with what you just read. Not optional. Not extra. This is Week 1."

The PDF design should match the course aesthetic — dark background, serif type, the same visual language. A cream-on-white PDF that looks like a printed worksheet breaks the experience.

### 11. Role of Companion Guides

The Companion Guide's role in the paid experience: it goes where the course content cannot. The course page introduces the concept of grief after late diagnosis. The Companion Guide gives the learner a private, unhurried space to go deeper — to name specific losses, to write without the feeling that they are inside a module.

The Guide should not repeat the course content. It should extend it into territory the course creates the doorway to but does not walk through.

For beta: the Guide needs to exist and be beautiful. A PDF with real writing in it. Not a fillable form. Writing from Leah, addressed to the learner, that goes deeper on the grief content than the course page does.

### 12. Role of live beta sessions

The beta sessions are the most powerful element of the current offer and they are almost invisible in the page design. They are mentioned in the "need support" card and in the nav as "Book a call" — but they should be a central feature of Week 1's experience.

For the learner: the live beta session is where the abstract content becomes concrete. It is where Leah sees them as individuals, where questions get answered in real time, where the recognition cards become real because other people in the session selected the same ones.

The Week 1 page should actively prepare the learner for the beta session: "This week's material is designed to be processed together in the beta session on [date]. Before you arrive: complete the Life Review Map Step 1 and name your recognition patterns."

## Essential for beta

These are the minimum requirements for the beta cohort to experience this as a paid course rather than a paid article.

1. **The video.** Even 8–10 minutes. Addressed to the beta cohort by name. Recorded before the first session.
2. **Learner outputs that save.** The recognition cards and the Life Review Map textarea from the prototype, brought into the production page.
3. **A single takeaway field at close.** "What am I carrying forward from this week?" with a textarea. Saves to localStorage.
4. **The Companion Guide as a real designed PDF.** Available to download. Introduced as a primary resource, not a footnote.
5. **The milestone at the end of the page, not buried.** One clear closing moment that acknowledges the learner has completed something.
6. **The beta session named on the page.** With a date, a purpose, and instruction on what to bring.

## Desirable later

These are meaningful improvements for the production course after beta.

1. **Personalised opening based on pre-course entry point.** A short intake question routes learners to a starting point appropriate to their state.
2. **Scrollytelling visual moment for the core concept.** One designed animation in Week 1 that visualises the reinterpretation of the past.
3. **Recognition card output feeding forward.** Selections from Week 1 named back in Week 2 context.
4. **Exportable Life Review Map.** Download as PDF at the end of the Apply section.
5. **Mobile-first low-capacity mode.** Mobile entry asks learner state and adjusts content display accordingly.
6. **Chaptered video with timestamps.** Learners can navigate directly to the concept most relevant to them.
7. **Companion Guide as a designed in-app reading experience.** Not just a PDF download.
8. **Progress indicator that reflects genuine engagement.** Not just page load — registers that the learner filled in a field, selected patterns, or completed the LRM Step 1.

## Gimmicks to avoid

1. **Gamification.** Points, badges, streaks, leaderboards. This audience has spent decades in systems that told them they were not trying hard enough. Gamification signals "you might fail" before the learner has done anything.
2. **Animated loading screens or entry experiences.** Neurodivergent learners may arrive in low-capacity states. Adding a gate before content is accessible is hostile.
3. **Social proof widgets during content.** "32 learners are reading this right now." This creates performance anxiety in solo reflective work.
4. **Auto-playing audio or video.** Always opt-in.
5. **Progress percentages.** "You are 34% through Week 1." For a learner with time blindness and task completion difficulties, a percentage that moves slowly is demoralising, not motivating.
6. **AI chat or coaching bot.** For emotionally significant psychoeducation content, a bot that simulates therapeutic conversation is not appropriate and potentially harmful.
7. **Comment sections or public reflection sharing.** The learner's grief and recognition patterns are private. Do not gamify sharing or create social pressure to disclose.
8. **Complex interactive diagrams that break on mobile.** The executive function visual in the prototype is a flex-wrap of boxes that collapses without hierarchy on mobile. Either design it for mobile first or use prose.

---

## What the learner leaves Week 1 with

If all essential elements are in place, a beta learner should leave Week 1 with:

1. **Named recognition patterns.** Eight potential patterns, which ones feel true for them, saved.
2. **At least one Life Review Map step completed.** A specific period of their life named and partially reinterpreted.
3. **A takeaway statement in their own words.** One sentence about what this week changed or named for them.
4. **The Companion Guide.** An acknowledgement that there is a place to go deeper if the grief landed heavily.
5. **Preparation for the beta session.** Knowing what they are bringing to the group.
6. **A different feeling about the category "late-diagnosed adult with ADHD" than they arrived with.** Not information. A shift.

The shift is the product. Everything else is infrastructure.
